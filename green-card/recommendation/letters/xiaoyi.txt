The field of computer science advances through the work of a small number of exceptional contributors, and it is on that basis that I write in strong support of Zilinghan Li. I am Xiaoyi Lu, an Associate Professor at the University of Florida, specializing in parallel and distributed systems, High-Performance Computing (HPC), Big Data analytics, and Artificial Intelligence (AI) architectures. My academic background includes a Ph.D. in Computer Science from The Ohio State University. Although I have not had a direct working relationship with Mr. Li, I am familiar with his contributions through the published literature and have directly cited and built upon his research in my own published works. As such, I have a highly informed perspective on both the technical substance and practical significance of his contributions.
I will speak first and most directly to my own engagement with Mr. Li’s work, as I believe it provides the clearest evidence of both the quality and impact of his research. My research group has cited and built upon two of Mr. Li’s contributions in two separate published papers. In my 2025 paper “FedDES: Discrete Event Based Performance Simulation for Federated Learning Systems”, published at the ACM/IEEE Symposium on Edge Computing, we needed rigorous baselines against which to evaluate our simulation framework. We selected FedCompass, Mr. Li’s algorithm for heterogeneous cross-silo federated learning, of which he is the first author, as a primary comparison baseline, specifically because it represents one of the most principled and effective solutions to the straggler problem in distributed AI training. The straggler problem is a fundamental bottleneck in real-world federated learning: when computing resources across participating institutions differ significantly, the entire training round is blocked waiting for the slowest client. FedCompass innovatively addresses this by dynamically estimating each client’s computing capacity and assigning a personalized workload so that all participants finish at roughly the same time. Selecting FedCompass as a key baseline was a deliberate engineering choice reflecting our assessment of it as a meaningful algorithmic advance. The same paper also cited the APPFL framework, which Mr. Li technically leads, as part of the federated learning infrastructure landscape.
In my most recent paper, “When RDMA Goes Long-Haul: Characterization, Modeling, and Verbs-Level Emulation with Implications for Federated Learning”, published at the 35th International Symposium on High-Performance Parallel and Distributed Computing (HPDC 2026), my group selected APPFL as the primary federated learning framework for the entire FL case study section of the paper. Specifically, we used APPFL v1.8.0 with its MPI backend, choosing it to represent high-performance FL deployments across multiple HPC clusters, to investigate how long-haul RDMA network conditions propagate through different FL aggregation strategies. We evaluated APPFL with 32 distributed clients across synchronous (FedAvg), buffered (FedBuff), and asynchronous (FedAsync) aggregation modes. APPFL’s depth and flexibility as a framework were essential to this study: it needed to support multiple aggregation strategies, run at scale across real distributed infrastructure, and integrate cleanly with MPI and UCX communication layers. This was not a peripheral reference, as APPFL was the experimental platform on which the central findings of the paper were built and validated.
