\begin{abstract}
The American Science Cloud (AmSC), established under the Genesis Mission of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), aims to integrate US's high-performance computing systems, experimental facilities, and data resources into a single, coordinated, AI-driven discovery platform. While early AmSC services emphasize the hosting and distribution of trained models and curated datasets, many scientific AI applications involve data that are institutionally siloed and cannot be centralized due to policy, privacy, or scale. Federated learning (FL) is the canonical example. In this paper, we argue that cross-facility FL is both a natural early workload for AmSC and a rigorous validation of its federated infrastructure. Using the Advanced Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning framework (APPFL) as a concrete reference implementation, we map the components of a production FL system, including aggregation services, distributed trainers, cross-site communication, and privacy mechanisms, onto the emerging AmSC service model. This mapping reveals gaps between current AmSC capabilities and the requirements of iterative, multi-facility learning workflows. We identify the platform services and research directions required to address these gaps, and argue that treating FL as a first-class AmSC workflow would benefit both privacy-preserving science and the platform's own development.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Scientific machine learning increasingly depends on data that cannot be moved. Experimental facilities generate petabyte-scale instrument data with strict residency requirements; biomedical collaborations operate under privacy regulation; industrial partners contribute proprietary measurements under restrictive agreements. In all of these settings, the classical workflow of ``gather the data, then train the model'' is not merely inconvenient but prohibited. Federated learning (FL)~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,kairouz2021advances} inverts this workflow: the model travels to the data, local updates are computed in place, and only model parameters or gradients are exchanged and aggregated. FL is therefore not simply a machine learning technique; it is an inherently \emph{multi-facility scientific workflow}, with all the orchestration, fault-tolerance, identity, and data-management challenges that the workflows community has long studied.

Concurrently, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has launched the Genesis Mission, a national initiative to connect its computing, data, and experimental facilities into a unified AI-driven discovery system. Its enabling platform is the American Science Cloud (AmSC), created under Section 50404 of Public Law 119-21 and seeded through an initial \$150M in expedited grants, with subsequent investments exceeding \$320M announced in late 2025~\cite{doe2025investments,obbb2025}. AmSC is envisioned as ``a system of United States government, academic, and private-sector programs and infrastructures utilizing cloud computing technologies to facilitate and support scientific research, data sharing, and computational analysis''~\cite{obbb2025}, realized in practice as a federated, cloud-like service layer over existing DOE HPC facilities and the Energy Sciences Network (ESnet). Early services emphasize hosting and distributing AI models and curated datasets, including inference-as-a-service capabilities in which trained models are stored centrally and executed on DOE HPC resources~\cite{doe2025investments}.

This host-and-distribute model, while valuable, presumes that either the data or the trained model can be centralized. We argue that the workloads AmSC is ultimately mandated to serve, i.e., cross-institutional scientific AI spanning national laboratories, universities, and industry, will frequently violate this presumption, and that AmSC should treat \emph{federated training} as a first-class workflow alongside centralized training and inference. FL is, in a precise sense, the workload AmSC's architecture already implies: geographically distributed sites connected by ESnet, heterogeneous compute allocations, institutional trust boundaries, and secure enclaves for elevated-security data.

To ground this position, we use APPFL, the Advanced Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning framework developed at Argonne National Laboratory~\cite{ryu2022appfl,li2025advances}, as a reference implementation of production cross-silo FL. APPFL was designed for exactly the deployment regime AmSC targets: FL across HPC centers, campus clusters, and cloud resources, using communication backends such as gRPC and Globus Compute~\cite{chard2020funcx} that traverse institutional boundaries, with authentication handled through federated identity. Mapping APPFL's architecture onto AmSC's emerging service model lets us be concrete about what AmSC already provides, what it nearly provides, and what is missing.

The primary contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we articulate the position that FL is a natural early workload and stress test for AmSC (Section~\ref{sec:position}). Second, we present a component-level mapping between a production FL framework (APPFL) and the AmSC service model (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}). Third, we derive from this mapping a concrete gap analysis and research agenda spanning model-exchange services, federated identity and authorization, scheduling, and cross-facility observability (Section~\ref{sec:gaps}).

\section{Background}
\subsection{The American Science Cloud}
AmSC (pronounced ``am-sec'') was established in 2025 as the platform-infrastructure cornerstone of the Genesis Mission~\cite{doe2025investments}. Distinct from commercial cloud offerings, AmSC is being constructed as a federated software layer over DOE Office of Science (SC) computing facilities, coordinated by Oak Ridge National Laboratory with SC laboratories participating as infrastructure partners~\cite{amsc2025call}. Its stated technical scope includes: (i) a cloud-like application programming interface (API) layer over Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR) HPC and networking facilities; (ii) hosting and distribution of AI models and AI-ready scientific datasets; (iii) inference-as-a-service on DOE HPC systems; (iv) common workflow and data-transfer tooling shared across facilities; and (v) secure enclaves for elevated-security workloads and data~\cite{amsc2025call,doe2025investments}. A companion effort, the Transformational AI Models Consortium, develops the models that this infrastructure will host, with AmSC providing infrastructure and the consortium consuming it~\cite{doe2025investments}.

Two aspects of this design bear directly on our argument. First, AmSC relies on existing ESnet connectivity rather than new network infrastructure, so multi-facility workloads must operate within the bandwidth and latency characteristics of the current wide-area network. Second, the early service catalog is oriented toward \emph{artifacts at rest}: models and datasets that are curated once and served many times. Iterative workloads, in which artifacts are repeatedly produced, exchanged, and consumed across facilities within a single logical job, have no counterpart in this catalog.

\subsection{APPFL: Cross-Silo Federated Learning for Science}
APPFL~\cite{ryu2022appfl,li2025advances} is an open-source federated learning framework designed for cross-silo scientific deployments. Its architecture is organized into four components with distinct responsibilities. The \emph{server}, or aggregator, implements aggregation strategies ranging from synchronous FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017communication} to asynchronous and heterogeneity-aware schedulers such as FedCompass~\cite{li2024fedcompass}, which explicitly accounts for disparities in client computing capacity---a common condition when clients are HPC allocations at different facilities. The \emph{client}, or trainer, encapsulates local training and is independent of the surrounding orchestration. The \emph{communicator} abstracts the transport layer, supporting the Message Passing Interface (MPI) for single-facility deployments, gRPC for point-to-point cross-site exchange, and Globus Compute~\cite{chard2020funcx} for federated function execution across administrative domains, with authentication provided by Globus-based federated identity. The \emph{privacy layer} applies differential-privacy mechanisms and secure aggregation protocols to the exchanged model updates. In addition, APPFLx~\cite{hoang2025enabling} provides a hosted service layer through which institutions join federations via web-based authentication, reducing the operational burden of cross-institutional studies in domains such as biomedicine.

APPFL is therefore representative of the requirements of any production FL system deployed on AmSC: it currently addresses cross-boundary transport, identity management, and client heterogeneity through mechanisms internal to the framework. A central question of this paper is which of these mechanisms should instead be provided by the platform


\section{Position: Federated Learning Is the Workload That AmSC's Architecture Implies}
\label{sec:position}
Our position rests on three observations.

\textbf{(1) FL follows from AmSC's mandate.} AmSC's statutory definition centers on cross-sector data sharing and computational analysis under legal, regulatory, and privacy constraints~\cite{obbb2025}. Where these constraints permit data movement, centralized training on hosted datasets suffices. Where they do not---as with protected health information, export-controlled measurements, and proprietary industrial data---federated training is the only workflow capable of fulfilling the mandate. A platform whose stated purpose includes privacy-constrained collaboration, but whose services support only centralized artifacts, would be incomplete by its own definition.

\textbf{(2) FL exercises the most demanding AmSC subsystems.} A single federated training job engages nearly every subsystem that AmSC is expected to provide: federated identity and authorization across laboratory boundaries; repeated wide-area transfers of medium-sized artifacts (model updates ranging from megabytes to gigabytes) under latency constraints; co-scheduling of short, recurring computations at multiple facilities; secure enclaves for clients holding elevated-security data; and end-to-end monitoring across administrative domains. Batch inference, by contrast, engages few of these. Consistent with the established practice of using driver applications to guide infrastructure design, adopting FL as an early workload would expose integration deficiencies while the platform architecture remains open to revision.

\textbf{(3) The FL community requires the infrastructure AmSC is constructing.} Conversely, scientific FL deployments are operationally fragile today precisely because each framework independently reimplements platform-level functionality: identity management, transport, endpoint management, and monitoring. If these capabilities were provided at the platform level, FL frameworks could concentrate on their scientific contributions---aggregation strategies, privacy mechanisms, and training logic---while relying on hardened, shared infrastructure. The mapping presented in the following section makes this division of responsibilities explicit.

\subsection{Model Hosting as the Federation's Shared State}
APPFL's server maintains the authoritative global model between training rounds. On AmSC, this state maps to a versioned platform artifact: the global model is checkpointed at each round, and provenance records link each version to the participating clients and the aggregation metadata. Current AmSC model services do not support this pattern: the Model Access Gateway provides authenticated access to hosted models for inference, but no user-writable artifact store~\cite{amscdocs2026}. Federated training requires read/write access to versioned artifacts with append-only lineages; the round-level provenance this produces also aids reproducibility. The High Performance Data Facility, whose early-access system is being designed around AmSC data services~\cite{doe2026scac}, could host such a store.

\subsection{From Inference-as-a-Service to Aggregation-as-a-Service}
AmSC's inference services execute hosted models on DOE resources in response to authenticated requests. Aggregation is likewise a function invoked on demand---when a quorum of client updates arrives---but it is stateful across rounds and executes user-supplied code rather than platform-curated models. We propose \emph{aggregation-as-a-service}: a platform service with which aggregation strategies (FedAvg, asynchronous variants, FedCompass-style scheduling~\cite{li2024fedcompass}) are registered and executed on updates deposited by authorized clients. Frameworks such as APPFL would contribute strategies as plugins instead of operating standalone servers, and collaborations without a facility to host an aggregator could still participate.

\subsection{Client Execution and Scheduling}
FL clients at HPC facilities run today as batch jobs or Globus Compute endpoints that the framework must keep available across rounds. Batch schedulers are designed for few long-running jobs; federated training consists of many short, recurring ones. Round-completion time is therefore dominated by queue delays and straggling clients rather than by computation or transfer. Recurring-reservation or event-triggered execution primitives in AmSC's API layer would allow a federation to specify that local training at a facility runs whenever a new global model version appears---a platform-level contract in place of per-site workarounds.

\subsection{Communication and Identity}
APPFL's communicator moves model updates across institutional boundaries
over gRPC or Globus Compute; each deployment negotiates firewall
traversal and authentication separately. A platform
\emph{model-exchange service}, providing authenticated transfer of model
artifacts between registered federation members over ESnet, would
replace the component of cross-silo FL that fails most often in our
deployments. The service needs federation-scoped authorization: a client
may deposit updates and retrieve global models only for federations to
which it belongs. One integration problem is already visible. APPFL and
APPFLx authenticate with Globus federated
identity~\cite{hoang2025enabling}, whereas AmSC's identity
infrastructure uses a platform identity provider with project-scoped
tokens~\cite{amscdocs2026}. The two regimes must interoperate before
existing FL deployments can adopt platform services.

\subsection{Privacy Mechanisms and Secure Enclaves}
AmSC's secure enclaves protect data and workloads at rest within a facility. FL adds protocol-level requirements: differential privacy applied to model updates, and secure aggregation, which prevents the aggregator itself from inspecting individual contributions. The two can be combined: executing aggregation-as-a-service inside an enclave gives federations a hardware trust anchor when members do not fully trust the hosting facility, alongside cryptographic protections.

\subsection{Cross-Facility Observability}
Cross-facility federated training constitutes a distributed workflow for which no complete view of execution state currently exists. Each facility monitors only the jobs executing on its own resources, while the FL framework captures only the events recorded in its own logs; consequently, the global state of a training run, including round progression, per-client staleness, communication timings, and failure attribution, cannot be reconstructed by any single party. The early AmSC service catalog does not address this requirement. Our development and operation of HiveWatch (\href{https://github.com/APPFL/hivewatch}{https://github.com/APPFL/hivewatch}), an observability toolkit for APPFL-based federations, has shown that the availability of such cross-site telemetry is a determining factor in whether failures within a federation can be diagnosed and attributed. We therefore contend that cross-facility observability must be provided as a platform capability rather than reimplemented as tooling within each FL framework.

\section{Gaps and Integration Objectives}
\label{sec:gaps}
The mapping in Section~\ref{sec:mapping} identifies four gaps, marked
G1--G4 in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}. We frame each as a near-term
research and integration objective appropriate to the current, formative
phase of AmSC.

\textbf{G1: Bidirectional, iterative model exchange.} The first objective is to evolve model hosting from read-oriented distribution into versioned, federation-scoped storage that supports both read and write access with round-level provenance. Several questions accompany this extension: which artifact granularity, whether full models, deltas, or compressed updates, is appropriate for repeated exchange over ESnet; what provenance schema adequately captures a training lineage; and how responsibility for such a store should be divided between AmSC model services and the High Performance Data Facility.

\textbf{G2: Aggregation-as-a-service.} The second objective is a platform interface for pluggable aggregation, governed by quorum, staleness, and scheduling policies. Realizing this interface raises questions of multi-tenancy and isolation between federations, of the execution model for user-supplied aggregation code, of support for asynchronous and heterogeneity-aware strategies~\cite{li2024fedcompass}, and of enclave-hosted execution for federations with limited mutual trust.

\textbf{G3: Federation-scoped identity and authorization.} The third objective concerns authorization semantics within AmSC's identity infrastructure that can express FL roles, namely aggregator, client, and observer, together with federation membership. Because existing deployments authenticate through Globus-based mechanisms~\cite{hoang2025enabling}, these semantics should interoperate with Globus identity so that current federations can migrate to platform services incrementally.

\textbf{G4: Cross-facility workflow observability.} The final objective is a standardized telemetry format for federated training rounds, covering round events, client participation, update provenance, and communication timings, integrated with facility-level monitoring so that operators and scientists share a single view of a federation's state.



\section{Related Work}
Cross-silo FL frameworks beyond APPFL, including FedML, Flower, NVIDIA FLARE, and OpenFL, similarly re-implement transport, identity, and orchestration internally; our argument for platform absorption applies to them equally, and a platform-level solution would benefit the ecosystem rather than a single framework~\cite{kairouz2021advances}.  The workflows community has long advocated driver applications for hardening multi-facility infrastructure~\cite{da2021community,da2024workflows}, and recent work on FAIR principles for HPC-resident AI artifacts~\cite{wilkinson2025applying} anticipates the provenance requirements of G1. Within DOE, the Integrated Research Infrastructure program~\cite{miller2023integrated}, on which AmSC research services build~\cite{doe2026scac}, identified cross-facility workflow patterns that FL instantiates in a particularly demanding form. To our knowledge, this is the first work to analyze FL as a workload for AmSC specifically.

\section{Conclusion}
The American Science Cloud is being built now, and the workloads chosen to drive its formative phase will shape its architecture for a decade. We have argued that cross-facility federated learning is both mandated by AmSC's purpose and uniquely effective at stress-testing its federated infrastructure, and we have used APPFL to make the required platform capabilities concrete: versioned model exchange, aggregation-as-a-service, federation-scoped identity, and cross-facility observability. Closing these gaps would simultaneously mature the platform and unlock privacy-constrained scientific collaborations that no host-and-distribute service can serve. We invite the workflows and AmSC communities to treat federated training as a first-class multi-facility workflow, and we offer the mapping and agenda here as a starting point.
