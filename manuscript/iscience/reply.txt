Response to Reviewers
We thank the Editor and Reviewers for their thoughtful and constructive comments. In revising the manuscript, we focused particularly on reframing the study around physician referral as the clinical endpoint, appropriately tempering interpretation of model performance, and more explicitly describing the limitations of the retrospective study design. Below, we address the major themes raised across the reviews.
1. Clarification of the clinical endpoint and study framing
A central concern raised by the reviewers was that physician referral for genetic testing is not equivalent to confirmed genetic aortopathy or molecular diagnosis. We agree that referral practices may reflect clinician judgment, documentation practices, and local workflows in addition to underlying disease status. We have revised the manuscript throughout to make this distinction explicit.
●	The Abstract now explicitly states that “physician referral for genetic testing served as the operational outcome label.”
●	The Methods further clarify that “physician referral for genetic testing was used as the operational ground-truth label, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making regarding suspected genetic disease rather than confirmed molecular diagnoses.”
●	We have revised the Discussion to consistently describe the framework as supporting genetic testing referral decisions, rather than diagnosing genetic aortopathy.
●	We also added a new Limitations of Study section in the Discussion stating: “Physician referral for genetic testing was used as the ground-truth outcome label since the objective of this study was to model the clinical decision to refer patients for genetic evaluation rather than to predict confirmed molecular diagnoses. While this framing aligns with the intended use of the system as a decision-support tool, referral practices may vary across clinicians and health systems and therefore may partially be reflective of local behavior and policy.”
2. Study limitations, clinical applicability, and generalizability
Reviewers also raised concerns regarding the scope of the study, clinical applicability, and generalizability beyond a single institution. We agree that the current study represents a preliminary clinical decision-support framework and have revised the manuscript to reflect this more appropriately.
●	We added a Limitations of Study section addressing scalability and future validation. This section states: “Future work will be needed to evaluate portability and performance across institutions. Adapting to other disease domains, such as stroke risk or cardiac adverse event, requires tailoring the corpus and prompt, accordingly. These considerations highlight the need for disease-specific adaptation and prospective evaluation before applying the framework to other clinical domains.”
●	We have substantially tempered claims of generalizability and explicitly characterize the current work as a single-institution feasibility evaluation rather than an externally validated clinical system.
●	The Methods describe how the architecture could be adapted for future validation and replication by adjusting the base model, prompt, retrieval database, and confidence threshold. We also clarify that larger model variants could be incorporated within the same pipeline when computational resources permit.
●	We explicitly state in the Limitations that “External validation is needed in subsequent work to assess performance in other clinical populations and across other disease domains.”
3. Interpretability and explainability
Reviewers raised concerns regarding the scope of the explainability analysis. We agree that the attribution experiments provide a technical exploration of model-input influence but do not establish clinically validated explanations or demonstrate applicability at scale.
●	We added a new Explainability Exploration section in the Results to provide greater context for the scope of these experiments.
●	We clarify in the Limitations that “In the future, this approach can be scaled to be useful in explaining some of the factors that influenced the model to make certain recommendations. Additional work is needed to scale this explainability approach across the entire dataset; compute time and resource usage were limitations for this analysis (Supplementary Table 2).”
●	Throughout the Methods and Discussion, we have changed references to “reasoning” outputs from the attribution analysis to “explanatory rationale” to more accurately describe the model-generated output without implying that the attribution analysis provides a faithful representation of model reasoning.
4. Interpretation of performance, false negatives, and clinical utility
Reviewers raised concerns regarding interpretation of the reported performance, particularly the 42 false-negative predictions and the use of a balanced retrospective cohort.
●	We revised the Results to explicitly report the patient-level confusion matrix: “207 patients were correctly determined by the model to require genetic testing (true positives), 41 were incorrectly thought to need testing when they did not (false positives), 209 were correctly identified as not needing testing (true negatives), and 42 were incorrectly classified as not needing testing when in fact they did (false negatives).”
●	We now explicitly state: “The false negative rate underscores that the system should be viewed as a supplemental flagging mechanism accompanying clinician assessment.”
●	We added an explicit limitation regarding prevalence: “Because the evaluation cohort was balanced between referral-positive and referral-negative patients, the observed precision should not be interpreted as a population-level positive predictive value; performance may differ substantially in clinical populations with different referral prevalence.”
●	We also acknowledge that pre-referral documentation may contain clinical suspicion cues. The Limitations state: “although the clinical notes selected for analysis were restricted to those that occurred prior to the date of the genetic testing order, documentation preceding the referral may still contain clinical suspicion cues that could influence the predictions of the model.” Accordingly, we frame the reported metrics as performance on a retrospective referral-prediction task, rather than as evidence of clinical deployment readiness.
