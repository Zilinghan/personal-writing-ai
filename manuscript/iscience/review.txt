Conceptual advance (seen by authors, reviewers, and editors)
Please use this section to indicate what the main findings of the paper relate to and advance the current state of the field. You may also use this section to comment on the methodology, design, and resources employed, whether the conceptual advance is of broad interest, the findings have the potential to significantly move the field forward, and how valid is the description of the study.

Reviewer #1: This manuscript presents an end-to-end open-source LLM pipeline (Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct) with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to identify undiagnosed rare genetic aortopathy patients from unstructured clinical notes, achieving 0.834 accuracy and 0.832 F3-score on 22,510 notes from 500 patients, with fully local deployment preserving data privacy.
The work addresses a critical gap between physician-dependent referral (often >5-year delay), rigid rule-based EHR systems, and proprietary LLMs with insurmountable privacy barriers. Key innovations include confidence-selective RAG (triggered only below 0.5 probability threshold), consensus-based longitudinal aggregation (0.4 threshold mimicking cumulative clinical evidence), and systematic model selection favoring 8B over 70B variants for superior sensitivity and single-GPU deployability.
However, fundamental design flaws undermine conceptual advance. Using physician referral for genetic testing as ground truth—rather than molecular diagnosis—means the system learns referral patterns, not disease presence. With 50% of cases merely "suspected aortopathy" and controls potentially containing undiagnosed cases, label noise and reversal risk are substantial. The 12-month pre-referral window truncates critical longitudinal phenotypic patterns, while the 50-patient development set is underpowered for hyperparameter optimization.
Performance metrics are clinically overinterpreted. Without prevalence calibration, the 0.832 F3-score is misleading: at realistic <0.1% prevalence, positive predictive value would be abysmal despite 0.836 specificity. The 8.4% false negative rate (42/500) for life-threatening aortic dissection risk lacks clinical acceptability discussion. No comparison with structured criteria (revised Ghent standard) or human geneticists is provided.
RAG effectiveness is contradictory—Figure 1f shows "All RAG" degrades performance versus selective RAG, yet RAG remains central. The knowledgebase excludes classic diagnostic guidelines (ACC/AHA), and interpretability analysis uses only synthetic cases with prohibitive computational costs (152 minutes/sample), lacking clinical expert validation.
Generalizability is entirely unproven—single-center, single-disease validation cannot establish cross-institutional robustness across Epic/Cerner EHR variations, and cross-disease extension claims remain speculative without experimental evidence.
To achieve Med-level conceptual advance, authors must: (1) redesign ground truth using genetically confirmed subsets; (2) provide prevalence-adjusted PPV/NPV and honest screening feasibility discussion; (3) add external validation or EHR-style sensitivity analysis. Without these, the work remains an incremental engineering demonstration rather than paradigm-shifting clinical AI.
Reviewer #2: This manuscript by Singhal et al. describes an open-source large language model (LLM)-enabled pipeline for recommending genetic testing for rare genetic aortopathies using longitudinal free-text clinical notes. The authors integrate Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct with confidence-triggered retrieval augmented generation (RAG) based on a curated aortopathy-related knowledge corpus, and evaluate the workflow in 500 individuals from the Penn Medicine health system. Overall, the study addresses an important and clinically relevant problem: the under-recognition of patients who may benefit from genetic evaluation for heritable aortopathy. The emphasis on locally deployable open-source models, longitudinal aggregation across clinical notes, and interpretable recommendation outputs is a strength. However, several aspects of the study design and interpretation require clarification to better align the claims with the available evidence and to strengthen the clinical validity of the proposed decision-support tool.

Reviewer #1: This manuscript claims to demonstrate an open-source LLM-enabled pipeline for identifying undiagnosed patients with rare genetic aortopathies from clinical notes, achieving clinically meaningful performance suitable for deployment as a decision-support tool. However, critical gaps between the presented data and stated conclusions require substantial experimental revisions before publication.
Claim 1: The system identifies "undiagnosed patients" who would benefit from genetic testing
The data do not support this conclusion. The study uses physician referral for genetic testing as the operational ground-truth label, not confirmed molecular diagnosis. This means the system learns to replicate existing referral patterns rather than discover missed cases. The "undiagnosed" framing is misleading—patients in the case cohort were already suspected by physicians, with 50% merely labeled "suspected aortopathy" (Figure 3a). To support the conclusion of identifying truly undiagnosed patients, the authors must conduct a prospective validation where the pipeline is applied to patients never referred for genetic testing, with follow-up genetic testing to establish true positive and false positive rates. Alternatively, a nested case-control design using biobank samples with available sequencing data could identify genetically confirmed cases who were never clinically diagnosed, testing whether the system detects them retrospectively.
Claim 2: The pipeline achieves patient-level accuracy of 0.834 suitable for clinical deployment
This conclusion is inadequately supported because the metrics lack clinical context calibration. The reported sensitivity (0.831) and specificity (0.836) are presented without prevalence-adjusted positive predictive value (PPV). For rare diseases with <0.1% prevalence, even high specificity yields extremely low PPV, making broad screening impractical without severe pre-filtering. The authors must provide PPV and negative predictive value (NPV) calculations across realistic prevalence scenarios (0.01%, 0.1%, 1%, 5%) and compare these against established screening modalities. Additionally, the 8.4% false negative rate (42/500) for a life-threatening condition requires explicit harm-benefit analysis—how many aortic dissection events would this miss, and is this acceptable compared to current standard of care?
Claim 3: The confidence-based selective RAG improves diagnostic performance
The evidence for this claim is contradictory and incomplete. Figure 1f shows that applying RAG to all notes ("All RAG") degrades performance compared to the base model, while selective RAG at 0.5 threshold shows marginal improvement. The authors must provide detailed ablation studies: (a) performance breakdown by confidence decile showing exactly which notes benefit from RAG; (b) qualitative analysis of RAG-retrieved documents versus model reasoning to verify knowledge utilization; (c) failure mode analysis of high-confidence errors that RAG failed to correct. Without this, the RAG component appears to be an unjustified complexity addition.
Claim 4: The framework is generalizable to other disease domains and healthcare systems
This is entirely speculative. Zero cross-institutional validation, zero cross-disease testing, and no analysis of EHR system dependencies (Epic vs. Cerner templates) are provided. To support this conclusion, minimum requirements include: (a) external validation on at least one independent health system's data, even if small-scale (n>100); (b) cross-disease adaptation experiment applying the same architecture to one additional rare disease (e.g., hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia or Fabry disease) with appropriate corpus replacement; (c) template sensitivity analysis showing performance stability across different note structures and documentation styles.
Claim 5: The interpretability pipeline provides clinically meaningful transparency
The data support only technical feasibility, not clinical utility. All interpretability examples use synthetic cases (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure 2); no real patient attributions are shown. The computational cost (152 minutes per sample for perturbation-based methods, Supplementary Table 2) is clinically prohibitive. Required revisions: (a) attribution visualization for at least 5 real cases with de-identified notes; (b) expert clinician review of attribution maps rating clinical relevance and reasoning alignment; (c) time-complexity analysis with optimization path to <5 minutes per case for real-world deployment.
Critical Research Design Flaws:
First, the control group construction is methodologically unsound. Age/sex-matched BioBank participants without phenotypic matching may include undiagnosed cases, creating label contamination that artificially deflates specificity. Controls should be matched on relevant phenotypic features (e.g., height, cardiovascular history) or verified negative through sequencing where available.
Second, the 12-month pre-referral window lacks justification and may be overly restrictive. Aortopathy phenotypes accumulate over years; sensitivity analysis comparing 6-, 12-, 24-, and 36-month windows is required to establish optimal temporal scope.
Third, the development set (50 cases/50 controls) is inadequate for robust hyperparameter optimization. With consensus threshold, confidence threshold, RAG parameters (sub_k, total_k), and temperature all tuned on this small sample, overfitting is highly probable. The authors should use nested cross-validation or report performance variance across multiple development set resamplings.
Fourth, the fine-tuning failure (Supplementary Figure 1) is dismissed too casually. Both LoRA configurations collapsed to predicting all cases as positive, suggesting catastrophic forgetting or inappropriate learning rate scheduling. Given that fine-tuning is standard practice for domain adaptation, the authors must investigate whether this failure is specific to their corpus size (1,064 PDFs) or indicates deeper architectural incompatibility, and discuss implications for the broader "small open-source models for medicine" narrative.
Evidence of Potential Outcome Switching:
The primary metric selection warrants scrutiny. The authors justify F3-score prioritization based on "minimizing missed diagnoses," yet select a 0.4 consensus threshold that balances sensitivity and specificity rather than maximizing sensitivity. This threshold choice appears driven by performance optimization on the development set rather than clinical reasoning. If false negatives are truly prioritized, the operating point should be explicitly selected to achieve >95% sensitivity with acceptable specificity tradeoff, and the clinical consequences of false positives (unnecessary genetic counseling, anxiety, costs) must be quantified.
Missing Data and Transparency Issues:
The manuscript states that 1,790 notes (7.95%) failed to generate parseable outputs, yet provides no analysis of whether these failures are random or systematic. If failures correlate with note complexity, language non-English sections, or specific departments, this introduces selection bias. Required: failure pattern analysis and sensitivity testing with imputation methods.
The RAG knowledgebase composition is described qualitatively but not quantitatively. What proportion are review articles versus primary research? How frequently is the corpus updated? Are clinical guidelines (ACC/AHA) included? This information is essential for reproducibility and assessing knowledge freshness.

Reviewer #2: 1. The manuscript frames the pipeline as a tool to identify undiagnosed patients with rare genetic aortopathies. However, the positive cohort consists of patients who had already been referred by physicians for genetic testing, and physician referral is used as the operational ground-truth label. This is a pragmatic and clinically meaningful endpoint for modeling referral decisions, but it is not equivalent to confirmed genetic aortopathy or molecular diagnosis. Therefore, the present study more directly evaluates whether the model can approximate or anticipate referral decisions based on pre-referral clinical documentation, rather than whether it can independently identify truly undiagnosed patients.
This distinction is important for interpreting the main claims. Referral practices may vary across clinicians, specialties, institutions, and patient populations, and may reflect local documentation and care pathways as much as true disease status. The authors acknowledge this limitation, but the manuscript would benefit from a more explicit and consistent framing throughout the title, abstract, and discussion. If available, the authors should report downstream genetic testing outcomes in the referred cohort, including the proportions of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, variants of uncertain significance, and negative results. In addition, a clinically informative analysis would be to determine whether the model would have flagged patients earlier than the actual testing order date, and by how much. Without such evidence, claims related to “identifying undiagnosed patients” should be tempered toward “identifying patients who may warrant genetic testing evaluation” or “supporting genetic testing referral decisions.”

2. The authors restricted case notes to those occurring within 12 months before the genetic testing order, which helps reduce direct post-referral leakage. Nevertheless, pre-referral notes may already contain explicit clinical suspicion cues, such as “Marfan syndrome,” “Loeys-Dietz syndrome,” “connective tissue disorder,” “genetics referral,” “genetic testing,” or “rule out heritable aortopathy.” If such terms are present, the model may be detecting documented clinician suspicion rather than independently recognizing aortopathy-related phenotypes. This would be particularly relevant because the endpoint itself is physician referral.
A sensitivity analysis excluding notes containing explicit diagnosis-, genetics-, or referral-related terms would substantially strengthen the manuscript. This would help determine whether the model retains clinically meaningful performance when obvious referral cues are removed. Similarly, the definition of the control cohort should be discussed in greater depth. Controls were defined by absence of documented genetic testing referral and absence of predicted loss-of-function variants in aortopathy-associated genes, but this may not exclude individuals with pathogenic missense variants, variants of uncertain significance, relevant family history, or under-recognized aortic phenotypes. Additional characterization of controls, including available aortic imaging history, cardiovascular diagnoses, family history, and broader variant categories, would improve confidence in the negative labels. If such data are unavailable, this uncertainty should be more clearly acknowledged.

3. At the patient level, the pipeline produced 42 false negatives and 41 false positives. Because the stated goal is to reduce missed opportunities for genetic testing, false negatives are especially important from a clinical safety perspective. The manuscript reports aggregate performance metrics, but does not provide sufficient clinical characterization of the misclassified patients. A focused error analysis would be valuable to determine whether false negatives were enriched for particular disease subtypes, milder or atypical phenotypes, family-history-driven referrals, sparse documentation, fewer notes, or absence of explicit syndromic features.
Likewise, false positives should be clinically characterized rather than treated only as statistical errors. Some false-positive patients may have partial aortopathy-like features, other cardiovascular conditions, or documentation patterns that reasonably triggered the model. Conversely, others may reflect over-sensitive recommendations that would create unnecessary genetic testing and counseling burden. The case cohort also includes heterogeneous conditions, including aneurysmal disease, Marfan syndrome, arterial dissection, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, ACTA2-related disease, and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. These disorders differ in presentation, genetic etiology, testing indication, and documentation patterns. Subtype-specific performance, or at least the distribution of true positives and false negatives across disease categories, would clarify whether the model performs broadly across heritable aortopathy syndromes or is driven mainly by the most common categories.
This analysis could also be connected to guideline-relevant clinical features. For example, it would be helpful to know whether model performance depends on documented family history, aortic diameter or dissection history, age at onset, aneurysm location, syndromic features, or extra-aortic manifestations. Such information would make the results more interpretable to clinicians and would better define where the tool is likely to be useful or unsafe.

4. The ability to generate supporting reasoning is presented as a key advantage of the LLM-based workflow, and the authors further provide token-level attribution examples. However, attribution visualization and plausibility of generated text do not necessarily establish that the explanations are clinically valid. In a decision-support setting, incomplete or misleading rationales may affect clinician trust, especially if the model highlights nonspecific features or omits critical information such as family history or aortic imaging findings.
The manuscript would be strengthened by expert review of a representative subset of model-generated rationales by clinical geneticists or aortopathy specialists. This review should ideally include true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives. The goal would not necessarily be to manually review all 22,510 note-level outputs, but to assess whether the reasoning is medically appropriate, whether it aligns with accepted genetic testing indications, and whether the model generates unsupported or overconfident explanations. This would provide a stronger clinical basis for presenting the reasoning component as an interpretable and useful feature of the pipeline.

5. The study is based on a single health system, and the authors appropriately acknowledge that documentation practices and referral workflows may differ across institutions. This limitation is particularly important because the outcome label is physician referral for genetic testing. Referral decisions may be influenced by specialty access, insurance status, socioeconomic factors, race/ethnicity, ancestry, and local availability of genetics services. As a result, the model may learn and reproduce existing referral patterns, including potential under-referral in specific demographic groups, rather than purely identifying biological risk.
If available, demographic-stratified performance should be reported, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and ancestry, and potentially insurance or other access-related variables. At minimum, the Discussion should more explicitly address the risk that models trained on referral-based labels may encode existing disparities in genetic testing access. The authors should also frame the current results as single-center feasibility data and avoid implying broad clinical generalizability before external validation in independent health systems, including non-tertiary and community-care settings.

Minor issues (seen by authors, reviewers, and editors)
Please list additional points that would benefit being addressed, including text clarity that would strengthen the paper but, are not strictly necessary to prove its main claims.

Reviewer #1: The manuscript contains several areas where clarity, completeness, and presentation could be substantially improved without requiring new experimental data. These points, while not essential to prove the main claims, would strengthen the paper's readability, reproducibility, and impact if addressed during revision.
Writing and Organization
The Introduction and Discussion sections contain substantial redundancy regarding the privacy advantages of open-source models and the limitations of proprietary LLMs. These arguments are presented at least three times across the manuscript. Consolidating this into a single focused paragraph in the Introduction, with a brief cross-reference in the Discussion, would improve flow and reduce reader fatigue. Similarly, the rationale for selecting Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct appears in both the Model Selection section and Results section; these should be merged.
The abstract states the pipeline "successfully categorized 425 out of 499 patients, with one case requiring further clinician evaluation due to incomplete information." This phrasing is awkward and potentially misleading—it implies 74 patients were not categorized, yet the patient-level metrics are reported on 499 patients. Clarifying whether the 425 refers to correct categorizations versus total processed would prevent confusion.
Figure and Table Presentation
Figure 1 combines six subplots (a-f) with disparate information types—performance metrics, confusion matrices, threshold sensitivity analyses, and probability distributions—into a single overly dense figure. Splitting this into two or three separate figures would improve readability and allow larger font sizes for axis labels. The color scheme in Figure 1b uses orange and blue for note-level and patient-level comparisons, but these colors are not consistently maintained across other panels, creating visual discontinuity.
Figure 3a presents disease distribution as a pie chart, which is generally discouraged for precise data communication given human difficulty in comparing slice angles. A bar chart or table would more accurately convey the 50.0% no-aortopathy-suspected, 25.4% aneurysmal disease, and 11.6% Marfan syndrome proportions. The violin plots in Figure 3b-c lack clear median or quartile annotations, making it difficult to extract exact distributional characteristics.
Figure 5, while comprehensive, contains arrows and numbering that are difficult to follow in monochrome print. Adding distinct colors for the four main pipeline stages (vector database creation, LLM inference, confidence evaluation, consensus aggregation) would improve visual parsing.
Methodological Detail Gaps
The prompt engineering process is insufficiently documented. The authors mention that "requesting a JSON-formatted output produces more consistent results compared to open-ended generations" and that "providing few-shot notes significantly elevates false positive rates," but provide no systematic prompt variation results or the specific few-shot examples tested. A supplementary table listing all prompt variants evaluated, with corresponding performance metrics on the development set, would substantially improve reproducibility.
The semantic double-pass merging (SDPM) chunking strategy is described with parameters (similarity threshold 0.5, chunk size 512 tokens, skip window size 1), but the embedding model used for computing semantic similarity during chunking is not specified. Given that this is distinct from the MedCPT embedding used for retrieval, this omission hinders replication.
The confidence score derivation from "log probability of the tokens for recommended or not recommended" requires clarification. Is this the mean log probability across the output token sequence, the minimum, or the probability of the classification token specifically? Different formulations yield meaningfully different uncertainty estimates.
Statistical Reporting
The manuscript reports metrics to three decimal places (0.834 accuracy, 0.835 precision) without confidence intervals or standard errors. Given the 250-case/250-control test set, exact binomial confidence intervals should be provided for all primary metrics. For example, the 0.831 sensitivity (207/249 true positives, assuming 499 successfully processed) has a 95% confidence interval of approximately 0.779–0.875, which substantially changes interpretation of precision.
The patient-level confusion matrix (Figure 1b) shows 209 true negatives, 41 false positives, 42 false negatives, and 207 true positives, summing to 499. However, the text states 425 "successfully categorized" patients. The discrepancy between 425 and 499 should be explicitly explained—does "successfully categorized" mean correct classification, or does it refer to a different processing subset?
Supplementary Materials
The supplementary materials are referenced inconsistently. "Supplementary Note 2" is cited for fine-tuning details, but the main text also refers to "Supplementary Table 2" for computation time and "Supplementary Figure 1" for fine-tuning performance. A clear numbering system (Supplementary Note 1-3, Supplementary Table 1-2, Supplementary Figure 1-2) is present, but the in-text citations sometimes use generic references ("Supplementary Note 2") without specifying whether table or figure within that note. Standardizing to "Supplementary Note 2, Table 1" format would improve navigation.
The supplementary figure showing model interpretability examples (Supplementary Figure 2) is rotated 90 degrees in the provided PDF, making it extremely difficult to read. This should be corrected to portrait orientation.
Clinical Context and Terminology
The manuscript uses "aortopathy" as a blanket term without defining its scope for non-specialist readers. Given Med's broad readership, a brief sentence clarifying that this encompasses Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD) would improve accessibility. The supplementary gene-disease table (Supplementary Table 1) lists 54 gene-disease pairs but is not referenced in the main text; integrating this into the Introduction or Methods would demonstrate the genetic heterogeneity motivating the study.
The term "genetic testing recommendation" is used throughout, but the specific type of testing (panel sequencing, whole exome sequencing, Sanger sequencing for known familial variants) is never specified. Different test types have different diagnostic yields and costs; clarifying that the system recommends referral for clinical genetics evaluation rather than a specific test would be more accurate.
Ethical and Regulatory Nuances
While the main limitations section appropriately notes single-institution design, it omits discussion of documentation bias. Clinical notes reflect physician documentation practices, which vary by training, time pressure, and institutional culture. A brief acknowledgment that the system may perform differently in environments with sparser or more verbose documentation styles would strengthen the limitations discussion without requiring new data.
The "Declaration of Interests" form (pages 21-23 of the submitted PDF) indicates all authors declare no competing interests, which is appropriate. However, the form date (April 12, 2024) predates the manuscript submission date, suggesting it may be a template rather than submission-specific. Confirming the declaration is current would be a minor administrative correction.
Code and Data Availability
The manuscript states that "implementation code and documentation for the pipeline will be released on GitHub upon publication" and that the "vector database capturing the curated aortopathy knowledgebase will be released." Given the emphasis on open-source methodology and reproducibility, making these resources available at the time of peer review—through a private repository link or embargoed release—would strengthen the paper's credibility. The current "upon publication" framing is standard but less compelling than immediate availability for reviewer inspection.
The GitHub repository URL (https://github.com/madduri-lab/llm-aortopathy-flagging) is provided in the submission form but not in the main manuscript text. Adding this to the Methods or Data Availability section would improve accessibility.
Narrative and Impact Framing
The Discussion frames the work as providing "a potential decision-support tool to assist clinicians in earlier recognition," but does not address the implementation pathway. A brief paragraph on potential integration points—EHR-embedded alerts, batch processing of established patient panels, or point-of-care clinical decision support—would help readers envision translation without requiring new experiments.
The final sentence claims this work "lays the foundation for scalable and privacy-compliant integration of generative AI into routine care." This is aspirational; tempering to "demonstrates feasibility for" or "provides a proof-of-concept framework for" would align better with the limited validation presented and avoid overclaiming.
Technical Terminology Consistency
The manuscript alternates between "large language models (LLMs)" and "Large Language Models" with inconsistent capitalization. Standardizing to lowercase except when beginning sentences would improve polish. Similarly, "retrieval augmented generation" and "retrieval-augmented generation" appear with and without hyphenation; the hyphenated form is standard and should be used consistently.
The term "F3-score" is introduced without definition. While F1 is widely understood, F3 (weighting recall three times precision) is less common. A brief parenthetical explanation at first use would aid readers.
Citation and Reference Formatting
Reference 16 (Chimirri et al., 2025) is cited as "medRxiv (2025)" without a DOI or preprint number, making it difficult to locate. Given that this is a key comparator study on GPT-4o for rare disease diagnosis, providing full citation details is important. Reference 28 (Hu et al., LoRA) is incomplete, ending with "ICLR 1, 3" without page numbers or DOI.
The citation of "MinerU" (Reference 22) as a "state-of-the-art PDF parsing tool" is accurate but could note that this is a very recently released tool (2024), which may limit its accessibility to readers wishing to replicate the knowledgebase construction.
Final Presentation Note
The manuscript footer on each page reads "Powered by Editorial Manager® and ProduXion Manager® from Aries Systems Corporation." While this is a submission system artifact, it creates visual clutter in the reviewer-facing version. This will presumably be removed during production but is worth noting for the authors' awareness.

Transparency of reporting (seen by authors, reviewers, and editors)
Does the paper adequately report the clinical trial endpoints, methods, statistics, data, and code? Are data and code availability sufficient to evaluate the manuscript? If not, please elaborate.

Reviewer #1: This manuscript demonstrates partial transparency in reporting but has significant gaps in data accessibility, statistical completeness, and code availability that limit independent evaluation and reproducibility. While the authors make good-faith efforts to disclose methods and commit to future code release, the current state falls short of Med's standards for a study claiming clinical relevance.
Clinical Trial Endpoints and Study Design Reporting
The study is not a clinical trial but rather a retrospective case-control analysis using existing clinical data, which the authors appropriately characterize. However, the framing as a "validation" study creates expectations of rigorous endpoint definition that are not fully met. The primary endpoint—patient-level F3-score at consensus threshold 0.4—is reported clearly, but secondary endpoints are poorly defined. The manuscript mentions note-level metrics, processing time, and interpretability analyses without specifying which were pre-specified versus exploratory. Given the multiple comparisons across thresholds (0.1–0.9), temperatures (0.3 vs. 0.7), and RAG configurations, the lack of pre-registration or analysis plan raises concerns about selective reporting.
The case and control cohort definitions are reported with reasonable detail: 273 referred cases with 12-month pre-referral notes, 1,200 BioBank controls age/sex-matched, final test set of 250/250 randomly sampled. However, the random sampling procedure is not described—was this stratified by disease subtype? Simple random sampling from 273 cases would likely underrepresent rare subtypes (Loeys-Dietz 0.8%, ACTA2-related 0.8% in Figure 3a), yet the final test set shows 11.6% Marfan and 9.8% arterial dissection. Clarifying whether stratification was used, or acknowledging subtype representation limitations, would improve transparency.
The ground-truth definition requires more nuance. The authors state "physician referral for genetic testing was used as the operational ground-truth label, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making regarding suspected genetic disease rather than confirmed molecular diagnoses." This is honest but buried mid-Methods; moving this limitation to the Abstract and emphasizing it in the Discussion would prevent misinterpretation by readers scanning quickly.
Methods Reporting
The pipeline architecture is comprehensively described in Figure 5 and accompanying text, with sufficient detail for conceptual replication. Key parameters are reported: Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct at temperature 0.7, confidence threshold 0.5, consensus threshold 0.4, sub_k=4 and total_k=25 for RAG retrieval. The semantic chunking parameters (similarity threshold 0.5, 512 tokens, 25-token overlap) are specified. However, several methodologically critical details are omitted or underreported.
The system prompt content is partially shown in Figure 6 but not provided in full text, making exact replication impossible. The JSON output schema is described but not shown, and the parsing logic for handling malformed outputs is not detailed. The "up to 3 rerun iterations" for failed notes is mentioned without specifying the rerun mechanism—identical prompt, temperature perturbation, or modified formatting instructions.
The RAG retrieval and ranking logic lacks granularity. The manuscript states BM25 and MedCPT are combined via reciprocal rank fusion, but the fusion formula (standard RRF with k=60? weighted combination?) is not specified. The embedding model for semantic chunking during knowledgebase construction is not identified, creating ambiguity about whether this differs from the MedCPT retrieval embedding. The exact MedCPT model variant (query encoder vs. document encoder? fine-tuned version?) is unspecified.
The fine-tuning methodology, while relegated to Supplementary Note 2, is actually more transparent than many main-text methods. LoRA configurations (rank 16/32, scaling factors 32/64, target matrices), optimizer settings (AdamW, learning rate 2×10⁻⁵, weight decay 0.01, decay factor 0.85), and training duration (1 epoch, ~20 hours on A100) are all reported. The training failure is also transparently disclosed. However, the exact training corpus composition—proportions of PubMed Central papers, GeneReviews chapters, and textbook chapters—is not quantified, making it impossible to assess corpus balance.
Statistical Reporting
Statistical transparency is a notable weakness. All performance metrics are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, standard errors, or variance estimates. For a 250-case/250-control test set, exact 95% confidence intervals for sensitivity (0.831, 207/249) would be approximately 0.779–0.875 using the Wilson score interval, and for specificity (0.836, 209/250) approximately 0.784–0.878. These intervals substantially overlap and would temper the precision of comparative claims. The lack of confidence intervals is particularly problematic given that the development set (50/50) was used for threshold optimization, yet no cross-validation stability metrics are provided.
The consensus threshold selection process (Figure 1c-d) shows performance curves but does not indicate whether these represent single-point estimates or averaged across multiple development set resamplings. With only 50 cases and 50 controls, threshold selection is highly susceptible to sampling variability. Bootstrap confidence bands around these curves would demonstrate whether 0.4 is genuinely optimal or merely a local maximum.
No statistical tests for comparing model variants are reported. Figure 4a-b shows multiple LLM performances, but whether Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct's apparent superiority is statistically significant versus Llama 3-8B-Instruct or Llama 3.1-70B-Instruct is untested. McNemar's test or paired permutation tests would be appropriate given the paired nature of predictions on the same patient set.
The note-level versus patient-level performance comparison lacks formal statistical linkage. The manuscript states aggregation "significantly improves" sensitivity from 0.630 to 0.831, but this is qualitative; a formal test of whether patient-level sensitivity exceeds note-level sensitivity, accounting for within-patient correlation, is not provided.
Data Availability
Data availability is severely limited, though understandably so given HIPAA constraints. The authors state "Patient notes data used in this study will not be made available to anyone due to HIPAA compliance and privacy restrictions for Penn Medicine." This is standard for clinical NLP studies but creates an irreproducibility barrier. The manuscript does not describe whether any de-identified data excerpts, synthetic examples, or data dictionaries can be shared, which would aid understanding without violating privacy.
The knowledgebase is promised for release ("Our vector database capturing the curated aortopathy knowledgebase will be released on GitHub upon publication"), which is commendable. However, the exact scope—1,064 PDFs—is reported without listing the specific documents, their publication dates, or update frequency. For a knowledge-dependent system, knowing whether the corpus includes 2024 guidelines or stops at 2019 is critical for assessing knowledge freshness. A supplementary file listing all included documents (title, source, year) would be valuable and does not raise privacy concerns.
No information is provided about whether the Penn Medicine BioBank data access can be requested by other researchers through formal channels. Many biobanks have controlled access procedures; noting whether such pathways exist would partially mitigate the non-availability statement.
Code Availability
Code availability is promised but not realized at the time of review. The GitHub repository URL (https://github.com/madduri-lab/llm-aortopathy-flagging) is provided in the submission form but not in the manuscript text. At the time of this review, the repository status is unknown—whether it contains placeholder content, full implementation, or is entirely private. For a study where reproducibility depends heavily on pipeline implementation details (prompt engineering, RAG orchestration, consensus aggregation logic), the inability of reviewers to inspect code during peer review is a significant limitation.
The authors commit to releasing "implementation code and documentation" and the "vector database" upon publication. This is standard practice but less transparent than immediate release with embargo, or provision of private access links to reviewers and editors. Given Med's emphasis on open science, requesting immediate private repository access for editorial review would be appropriate.
Specific code components whose absence hinders evaluation include: the exact prompt templates (system prompt, user prompt with note formatting, RAG-augmented prompt variant); the JSON parsing and error-handling logic for malformed LLM outputs; the confidence score extraction from token log probabilities; the consensus aggregation implementation; and the Captum interpretability pipeline configuration. Figure 6 shows prompt schematics but not executable text; the interpretability pipeline description lacks code-level detail sufficient for replication.
Reproducibility of Computational Environment
The computational environment is partially reported. Databricks cluster specifications are provided (15.4 LTS, standard NC24ads A100 v4 220GB, 1 worker), allowing rough cost estimation (~30/hourfor18hours=~540 total compute). However, the exact software versions (Python, PyTorch, Transformers, Captum, vector database library) are not listed. The LLM inference framework—whether raw Transformers, vLLM, Hugging Face TGI, or custom wrapper—is unspecified, and this choice substantially affects reproducibility given API differences across frameworks.
The RAG vector database technology is not identified (Chroma, FAISS, Pinecone, Weaviate, or other). Different vector stores have distinct indexing, retrieval, and embedding storage behaviors that could affect reproducibility even with identical corpora.
Missing Data and Attrition Reporting
The manuscript reports 1,790 failed notes (7.95%) but does not analyze failure patterns. Were failures random with respect to note length, department, patient case/control status, or note content characteristics? A supplementary table breaking failures by these dimensions would clarify whether the analyzed sample is representative. The single patient excluded (0.2%) due to having only one incomplete note is mentioned, but no characteristics of this patient are provided—were they a case or control? This matters for assessing whether exclusion introduces bias.
The development set usage is not fully transparent. The 50-case/50-control development set was used for model selection, temperature comparison, threshold optimization, and RAG parameter tuning. Multiple testing on the same small dataset without independent validation raises overfitting concerns. The manuscript does not report whether any patients in the development set appear in the test set, which would be a critical contamination error if present.
Outcome Reporting and Selective Analysis
The primary outcome (F3-score at threshold 0.4) is clearly designated, but the rationale for F3-weighting (β=3) is not rigorously justified. The authors state this prioritizes sensitivity over precision, but why β=3 specifically versus β=2 or β=5? A sensitivity analysis showing performance at β=1, 2, 3, 5 would demonstrate robustness of the metric choice.
Multiple secondary analyses are presented without clear pre-specification: note-level versus patient-level comparison, temperature comparison, RAG parameter comparison, model architecture comparison, interpretability analysis. While exploratory analyses are valuable, distinguishing pre-specified from post-hoc analyses would improve transparency. The interpretability analysis in particular appears entirely exploratory, yet occupies substantial Results space and figures.
The fine-tuning failure is transparently reported but not formally analyzed as an outcome. Given that continual pre-training is a standard domain adaptation approach, the complete failure of both LoRA configurations (collapsing to all-positive predictions) is an important negative result that merits deeper analysis—was the learning rate too high? Was the corpus too small relative to the model's pre-training scale? Did catastrophic forgetting occur? The current explanation ("limited data size and catastrophic forgetting") is hand-wavy rather than analytically demonstrated.
Comparison with Reporting Guidelines
For a study involving AI/ML in healthcare, the manuscript does not explicitly reference relevant reporting guidelines such as TRIPOD-AI, CONSORT-AI, or DECIDE-AI. While not strictly required, alignment with these frameworks would strengthen transparency. Key elements from these guidelines that are missing include: patient and public involvement statement, algorithm change protocol, error analysis by demographic subgroups, and human-AI interaction considerations.

Reviewer #1: This manuscript presents an open-source LLM-enabled pipeline for identifying patients with rare genetic aortopathies from clinical notes, achieving patient-level accuracy of 0.834 and F3-score of 0.832. While the work addresses an important clinical problem and demonstrates technical competence, I have significant concerns regarding validation rigor, clinical interpretability, and transparency that must be addressed before publication in Med.
Major Concerns
The primary weakness is the ground-truth definition. Using physician referral for genetic testing as your operational label means the system learns to replicate existing referral patterns rather than identify truly undiagnosed cases. This is particularly problematic given that 50% of your case cohort was merely "suspected aortopathy" rather than genetically confirmed disease. To support your claim of identifying "undiagnosed patients," you need to demonstrate that the system detects cases missed by standard care. I strongly recommend either a prospective validation component or a nested case-control analysis using biobank samples with available sequencing data to identify genetically confirmed but clinically undiagnosed cases.
Your performance metrics lack clinical context calibration. The reported 0.831 sensitivity and 0.836 specificity appear strong in isolation, but for rare disease screening with prevalence below 0.1%, the positive predictive value would be extremely low. You must provide prevalence-adjusted PPV and NPV calculations across realistic prevalence scenarios (0.01%, 0.1%, 1%, 5%) and discuss the clinical feasibility of your tool in real screening contexts. Additionally, the 8.4% false negative rate for a life-threatening condition like aortic dissection requires explicit harm-benefit analysis—is this acceptable compared to current standard of care?
The RAG component presents contradictory evidence. Figure 1f shows that applying RAG to all notes degrades performance versus selective RAG, yet you present RAG as a core innovation. Please provide detailed ablation studies showing exactly which notes benefit from RAG and why full RAG application is harmful. I also need clarification on your knowledgebase composition—do you include ACC/AHA guidelines, and how do you handle the temporal freshness of retrieved literature?
Your generalizability claims are entirely unsupported. Single-center, single-disease validation cannot establish cross-institutional robustness, especially given the known variability in EHR documentation styles across Epic, Cerner, and homegrown systems. At minimum, you should provide a sensitivity analysis on note characteristics (department, length, template usage) and ideally validate on external data. Your claims of cross-disease extensibility remain speculative without any experimental demonstration.
The interpretability analysis uses only synthetic cases. For a clinical decision support tool, I need to see real patient attributions validated by clinical experts. The computational cost of 152 minutes per sample for perturbation-based methods is clinically prohibitive—please discuss how you intend to make this scalable or whether you prioritize faster but potentially less precise attribution methods.
Minor Concerns
Please consolidate redundant discussions of open-source privacy advantages across Introduction and Discussion. Your abstract's statement that 425 of 499 patients were "successfully categorized" is ambiguous—does this mean correctly classified or merely processed? Clarify this and the discrepancy with your reported 499-patient confusion matrix.
Provide confidence intervals for all primary metrics using exact binomial methods. Report your software environment in full, including vector database technology, LLM inference framework, and package versions. Make your code and knowledgebase available for editorial review immediately, even if embargoed for public release until publication.
Consider replacing the pie chart in Figure 3a with a bar chart for more accurate proportion comparison. Split Figure 1 into multiple figures given its current density. Ensure Supplementary Figure 2 is presented in readable portrait orientation.
Overall Recommendation
This manuscript demonstrates proof-of-concept feasibility but not clinical viability. The conceptual advance is promising—privacy-preserving, locally deployable AI for rare disease screening—but current validation fails to bridge the gap between algorithmic performance and genuine patient benefit. I recommend Major Revision with the essential additions of: (1) prevalence-calibrated clinical utility analysis, (2) external or cross-disease validation evidence, and (3) real-case interpretability with expert validation.


Reviewer #2: This manuscript by Singhal et al. describes an open-source large language model (LLM)-enabled pipeline for recommending genetic testing for rare genetic aortopathies using longitudinal free-text clinical notes. The authors integrate Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct with confidence-triggered retrieval augmented generation (RAG) based on a curated aortopathy-related knowledge corpus, and evaluate the workflow in 500 individuals from the Penn Medicine health system. Overall, the study addresses an important and clinically relevant problem: the under-recognition of patients who may benefit from genetic evaluation for heritable aortopathy. The emphasis on locally deployable open-source models, longitudinal aggregation across clinical notes, and interpretable recommendation outputs is a strength. However, several aspects of the study design and interpretation require clarification to better align the claims with the available evidence and to strengthen the clinical validity of the proposed decision-support tool.
Major Concerns:
1. The manuscript frames the pipeline as a tool to identify undiagnosed patients with rare genetic aortopathies. However, the positive cohort consists of patients who had already been referred by physicians for genetic testing, and physician referral is used as the operational ground-truth label. This is a pragmatic and clinically meaningful endpoint for modeling referral decisions, but it is not equivalent to confirmed genetic aortopathy or molecular diagnosis. Therefore, the present study more directly evaluates whether the model can approximate or anticipate referral decisions based on pre-referral clinical documentation, rather than whether it can independently identify truly undiagnosed patients.
This distinction is important for interpreting the main claims. Referral practices may vary across clinicians, specialties, institutions, and patient populations, and may reflect local documentation and care pathways as much as true disease status. The authors acknowledge this limitation, but the manuscript would benefit from a more explicit and consistent framing throughout the title, abstract, and discussion. If available, the authors should report downstream genetic testing outcomes in the referred cohort, including the proportions of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, variants of uncertain significance, and negative results. In addition, a clinically informative analysis would be to determine whether the model would have flagged patients earlier than the actual testing order date, and by how much. Without such evidence, claims related to "identifying undiagnosed patients" should be tempered toward "identifying patients who may warrant genetic testing evaluation" or "supporting genetic testing referral decisions."
2. The authors restricted case notes to those occurring within 12 months before the genetic testing order, which helps reduce direct post-referral leakage. Nevertheless, pre-referral notes may already contain explicit clinical suspicion cues, such as "Marfan syndrome," "Loeys-Dietz syndrome," "connective tissue disorder," "genetics referral," "genetic testing," or "rule out heritable aortopathy." If such terms are present, the model may be detecting documented clinician suspicion rather than independently recognizing aortopathy-related phenotypes. This would be particularly relevant because the endpoint itself is physician referral.
A sensitivity analysis excluding notes containing explicit diagnosis-, genetics-, or referral-related terms would substantially strengthen the manuscript. This would help determine whether the model retains clinically meaningful performance when obvious referral cues are removed. Similarly, the definition of the control cohort should be discussed in greater depth. Controls were defined by absence of documented genetic testing referral and absence of predicted loss-of-function variants in aortopathy-associated genes, but this may not exclude individuals with pathogenic missense variants, variants of uncertain significance, relevant family history, or under-recognized aortic phenotypes. Additional characterization of controls, including available aortic imaging history, cardiovascular diagnoses, family history, and broader variant categories, would improve confidence in the negative labels. If such data are unavailable, this uncertainty should be more clearly acknowledged.
3. At the patient level, the pipeline produced 42 false negatives and 41 false positives. Because the stated goal is to reduce missed opportunities for genetic testing, false negatives are especially important from a clinical safety perspective. The manuscript reports aggregate performance metrics, but does not provide sufficient clinical characterization of the misclassified patients. A focused error analysis would be valuable to determine whether false negatives were enriched for particular disease subtypes, milder or atypical phenotypes, family-history-driven referrals, sparse documentation, fewer notes, or absence of explicit syndromic features.
Likewise, false positives should be clinically characterized rather than treated only as statistical errors. Some false-positive patients may have partial aortopathy-like features, other cardiovascular conditions, or documentation patterns that reasonably triggered the model. Conversely, others may reflect over-sensitive recommendations that would create unnecessary genetic testing and counseling burden. The case cohort also includes heterogeneous conditions, including aneurysmal disease, Marfan syndrome, arterial dissection, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, ACTA2-related disease, and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. These disorders differ in presentation, genetic etiology, testing indication, and documentation patterns. Subtype-specific performance, or at least the distribution of true positives and false negatives across disease categories, would clarify whether the model performs broadly across heritable aortopathy syndromes or is driven mainly by the most common categories.
This analysis could also be connected to guideline-relevant clinical features. For example, it would be helpful to know whether model performance depends on documented family history, aortic diameter or dissection history, age at onset, aneurysm location, syndromic features, or extra-aortic manifestations. Such information would make the results more interpretable to clinicians and would better define where the tool is likely to be useful or unsafe.
4. The ability to generate supporting reasoning is presented as a key advantage of the LLM-based workflow, and the authors further provide token-level attribution examples. However, attribution visualization and plausibility of generated text do not necessarily establish that the explanations are clinically valid. In a decision-support setting, incomplete or misleading rationales may affect clinician trust, especially if the model highlights nonspecific features or omits critical information such as family history or aortic imaging findings.
The manuscript would be strengthened by expert review of a representative subset of model-generated rationales by clinical geneticists or aortopathy specialists. This review should ideally include true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives. The goal would not necessarily be to manually review all 22,510 note-level outputs, but to assess whether the reasoning is medically appropriate, whether it aligns with accepted genetic testing indications, and whether the model generates unsupported or overconfident explanations. This would provide a stronger clinical basis for presenting the reasoning component as an interpretable and useful feature of the pipeline.
5. The study is based on a single health system, and the authors appropriately acknowledge that documentation practices and referral workflows may differ across institutions. This limitation is particularly important because the outcome label is physician referral for genetic testing. Referral decisions may be influenced by specialty access, insurance status, socioeconomic factors, race/ethnicity, ancestry, and local availability of genetics services. As a result, the model may learn and reproduce existing referral patterns, including potential under-referral in specific demographic groups, rather than purely identifying biological risk.
If available, demographic-stratified performance should be reported, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and ancestry, and potentially insurance or other access-related variables. At minimum, the Discussion should more explicitly address the risk that models trained on referral-based labels may encode existing disparities in genetic testing access. The authors should also frame the current results as single-center feasibility data and avoid implying broad clinical generalizability before external validation in independent health systems, including non-tertiary and community-care settings.
Minor Concerns:
1. The Results section states that a consensus threshold of 0.4 was selected as the primary operating point, whereas the Methods section states that if more than 50% of a patient's notes are classified as "recommended," the final patient-level output is set to "recommended." This apparent inconsistency should be corrected. The final threshold used for the reported test-set performance should be stated clearly and consistently.
2. The number of notes per patient varies substantially. A fixed consensus threshold may behave differently for patients with very few notes compared with patients with many encounters. The authors should provide performance stratified by note count, or at least discuss how sparse documentation affects patient-level aggregation. This is especially relevant because one patient failed to receive a final prediction due to having only one incomplete and uninformative note.
3. The manuscript reports that 1,790 of 22,510 notes failed to generate output in the desired format and were excluded from further analysis. It would be useful to report whether these failures were enriched in cases or controls, whether they were associated with note length or note type, and whether excluding them affected patient-level recommendations. This would improve transparency regarding the operational robustness of the pipeline.
4. Although prioritizing sensitivity is reasonable for rare disease screening, false-positive recommendations may lead to additional genetic counseling visits, testing costs, patient anxiety, incidental findings, and VUS interpretation burden. The Discussion would benefit from a more explicit consideration of the expected clinical workload and downstream consequences if the pipeline were deployed at scale.


Reviewer #3: Rare genetic aortopathies, such as Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome, can lead to aortic complications and in practice, diagnosis can be delayed by many years which can lead to early death. In this peice, the authors evaluate whether LLMs can support identification of patients who may benefit from genetic testing based on clinical narrative data.

They have a dataset of 500 patients (250 cases and 250 controls) with 22,510 clinical notes in total within the Penn Medicine's health system. The system generates a note (i.e., enoucnter)-level recommendation with modest performance (sensitivity of 0.630, precision of 0.242). They then aggregatied predictions across all notes per patient using a consensus-based aggregation strategy, in which a patient was flagged if the proportion of notes recommending genetic testing exceeded a consensus threshold. As expected this boosted performance:
207 patients were correctly determined by the model to require genetic testing (true positives), 41 were incorrectly thought to need testing when they did not (false positives), 209 were correctly identified as not needing testing (true negatives), and 42 were incorrectly classified as not needing testing when in fact they did (false negatives).



Comments:
1) When setting up a test system for something as severe as genetic aortopathy and aortic dissection, surely it is better to maximize sensitivity? Having roughly 17% of the predictions be false negative (42 out of the 207+42) is surely not appropriate, especially given the costs of genetic testing (exome or genome) are now so much lower than a decade ago? What is the danger that using a system like this will somehow lead physicians not to test patients who actually have a genetic aortopathy, potentially increasing the risk of acute dissection? Still, the fact that there is a high sensitivity 12 months before the test was ordered suggests potential utility if deployed in a responsibly way. (In other words, I think that the following sentence in the discussion is clinically wrong and potentially dangerous: " A threshold of 0.4 was therefore selected as it achieved near-equivalent sensitivity and specificity, reflecting a balanced operating point that does not disproportionately sacrifice sensitivity for precision").

2) Recent literature on medical uses of LLMs implicitly assumes that everything that came before LLMs has been made obsolete. In any other branch of bioinformatics or translational informatics, it would be necessary to test any new method against what came before. LLMs can make many things easier to set up (basically, just extract texts/data from source, write a prompt, and send to the LLM), but still it would be interesting to know more about the data and how well a simple scoring system would perform. Presumably, the LLMs are picking up on either physical exam findings (e.g., of Marfan syndrome, which are age dependent but often fairly characteristic) or on some suggestive cardiovascular finding.
So it would be really important for the authors to provide an overview on the findings in the patients:
- Ghent-nosology-related physical exam findings
- Any available cardiology findings especially echocardiography with examination of the aorta
- Body height (Z score)
These are things that could be extracted from the records by an LLM. This would be important to understand if physicians missed well recognized early signs of aortopathies or whether the LLMs are picking up some more complicated patterns (which would be interesting to investigate, recognizing that this is difficult, but some analysis can surely be done)

3) The interpretability pipeline does not seem to be adding true interpretability in the example shown. The tool is text-mining medical concepts from an input text (which can also be done with standard text mining). The patient has symptoms that appear to reflect IBD. If this was a typical negative case used in the training, then I would say this is not clinically realistic because there appears to be little that would suggest an aortopathy. The challenges encountered in Marfan clinics are often with patients who present with several skeletal features of the Ghent nosology and maybe a high normal aortic diameter at age 16 years -- it could go either way.
This is a general problem -- the results of the accuracy analysis will depend on the training and test datasets, and the authors do not provide a lot of details.

In the main text, the aithors write " In this case, we see relevant symptoms such as "chronic abdominal pain", "intermittent diarrhea", "recurrent hernias", and "fatal ruptured artery" score highly" -- however, in Figure 2 I do not see "fatal ruptured artery"?? In any case, after a "fatal ruptured artery" there is no more need for prediction, so this example is not very convincing.

4) Another issue is that the utility of LLM systems depend on the users (or on the input data). Penn is one of the best hospitals in the US. Presumably the greatest need for early warning systems is in average or even subaverage hospitals. It would be interesting to know if the system is generalizable to at least one other setting. Experience has shown that LLMs, especially local LLMs, often need quite a bit of fine tuning to perform well (e.g., the prompt may need to contain descriptions that are tailored to local formats or usages).
Given that Penn is a research hospital, I would guess that its EHR data are much better than those of many other hospitals? The authors should comment on these topics and ideally should test the system with some other dataset.

5) Black box systems
This goes beyond the scope of the current manuscript and this is an optional comment, but I am worried that if hospitals implement early warning systems that do not attempt to teach physicians to be better physicians but rather provide the physicians with black box recommendations, this will ultimately lead to a loss of physician skills and worsening of clinical care.

The authors write "Our vector database capturing the curated aortopathy knowledgebase will be released on GitHub upon publication to facilitate reproducibility and reuse"

It is unusual for the code not to be made available to reviewers.
